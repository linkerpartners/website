Five things brands did first when they entered Japan

Brands that started without a big budget had one thing in common: instead of translating, they first built what Japanese customers need to trust them.

They rewrote the Japanese page from scratch

Several Korean brands that did well on Qoo10 started by rebuilding their product pages for Japanese customers. One underwear brand changed its thumbnails and descriptions to suit Japanese shoppers' preference for information-rich images, and said traffic rose sharply.

Names matter too. One diet-food brand shortened its Korean-style product name to fit how similar products are named in Japan. Switching to a familiar word was faster than explaining an unfamiliar one.

They built trust first, with reviews and samples

The smaller the ad budget, the more reviews and hands-on experience mattered. One skincare brand put a demo video and trial reviews at the very top of its product pages and gathered reviews beforehand through sample events. Seeing that sampling is not an established habit in Japan, another brand put samples in every order and turned that into set purchases.

In another case, a voluntary post from an influencer with a modest following moved sales. What mattered was how well the person fit the product, not the follower count.

They put their weight behind one or two hero products

A select shop run by one person looked for products that would sell and focused on one or two hero items. It passed 30,000 followers in two years. For a brand entering for the first time, making one thing well known before adding more was the safer route.

Many did not aim at big retailers at the start. A bakery brand set up a Japanese entity, tested the response with a small pop-up, and used that response to move up to a department-store pop-up.

So where should you start?

Earlier notes

  • 1. Work out fees from one real product

    Qoo10 fees can vary by category and by the programmes you join. Check the official fees for your category, pick one product, and subtract fees, shipping and ad spend from its selling price to see what is left.

    Put exchange rates and transfer fees in the same sum, and you will rework your prices less often later.

    2. Shipping at the speed customers expect

    Customers in Japan tend to care about delivery dates and packaging. Shipping from Korea and shipping from a warehouse in Japan differ in time and cost, so decide which to start with. Write down the address and steps for returns and exchanges too, so you can answer questions straight away.

    3. Check the payout cycle and how you are paid

    Find out when, in which currency and by which route sales proceeds arrive. A long cycle means you need working capital to cover it, and transfer methods carry different fees. Sketch a payout calendar before you start selling and your cash planning gets easier.

    4. Build the product page in the order Japanese customers read

    Go beyond translating the description. Arrange it in the order Japanese customers want to know: how to use it, ingredients or materials, reviews, and shipping and returns. Check that ingredient and efficacy wording fits Japanese rules, and put the text inside images into Japanese as well, which makes reading much easier.

  • Change the order before the sentences

    Sentences from a translation tool can be grammatical and still read in an unfamiliar order. Rather than carrying over the order you used in Korea, move the information local customers want first to the top. Customers in Japan tend to look early for concrete details such as how to use the product, ingredients and reviews.

    Choose one tone of voice

    In Japanese, the level of politeness changes the impression a lot. Decide who your brand is speaking to and keep the same tone from the product name to the notices and the closing line. On Korean pages, smoothing stiff, translated phrasing into everyday language makes them easier to read.

    Localize the text and numbers inside images

    Wording inside images is often missed by translation tools. Sizes, units, dates and price formats also build trust when they follow local habits.

  • Sound and meaning

    When your name is read in katakana or the Latin alphabet, check that it does not sound like another word or feel awkward in Japanese. Ask a Japanese speaker to read it aloud and tell you what words come to mind. The same spelling can be read in different ways.

    The impression of colour

    The impression a colour gives can differ by country and by industry. Look at the colours Japanese brands in your field use, and check that your logo's colour gives the impression you intend.

    Lettering and layout

    Japanese is set both horizontally and vertically, and mixes kanji, hiragana and katakana, so letter shapes vary widely. When you add Japanese to a logo, adjust the weight and spacing of the Japanese typeface separately and check that it still reads at small sizes. Placed beside a Korean or Latin logo, matching height and visual weight keeps the pair steady.

  • Allowed efficacy claims differ by country

    For cosmetics and food, each country sets a range of effects you may claim. Wording that is common in Korea may not carry over to Japan as it is, and the reverse can also be true.

    Wording to check first

    Words that suggest medicine, such as “treat”, “prevent” and “cure”, effects with numbers, comparisons with other products, and claims backed by experts or doctors all need a careful check. For ordinary foods, the basic rule is to avoid wording that suggests preventing or treating disease.

    A checking order before you publish

    1. Confirm which category your product is sold under: cosmetics, ordinary food, food with function claims and so on.

    2. Look up the range of claims allowed for that category in official sources.

    3. Gather product pages, ad copy, text inside images and quoted reviews into one table and check them against it one by one.

    4. Replace borderline wording with plain facts such as ingredients, feel and how to use.

    This is general guidance, not legal advice. Rules change, so check the latest official sources or a specialist before you sell. LINKERPARTNERS localization also checks that wording fits the rules.

  • Test online first

    In Korea, searching for and comparing products on online channels such as Smartstore, Coupang and Instagram is very active. Starting small online and watching the response is a lighter load than rushing into physical stores.

    How fast reviews and photos build up

    Customers in Korea tend to read purchase reviews and photo reviews closely. Look after the first few reviews, and answer customer questions quickly. On a Korean product page, go beyond translating the Japanese original and put what Korean customers ask most near the top: ingredients, how to use it, delivery time and exchange methods.

    Test small, fix fast

    Plan your first four weeks around one or two products, a small ad budget and about two edits to the product page. Keep the wording and images that work and change the rest the following week; that pace suits the Korean market. A small pop-up or a test sale is also a good way to check the response in person.
